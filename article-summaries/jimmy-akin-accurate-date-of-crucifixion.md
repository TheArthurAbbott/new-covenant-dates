# The Accurate Date of the Crucifixion

Jimmy Akin (Catholic Answers) argues that seven clues from the Gospels and extra-Biblical sources converge on a crucifixion date of 3 p.m., Friday, April 3, AD 33, with Jesus's ministry beginning around AD 29, his birth in 3/2 BC, and Herod the Great's death in 1 BC. Akin is a proponent of the high sabbath falling on a standard (Saturday) sabbath, possibly reflecting Catholic tradition, so his dating of the crucifixion to a Friday may carry biased interpretation regarding the day-of-week question.

**Source:** https://www.catholic.com/audio/caf/the-accurate-date-of-the-crucifixion

---

## Death of Herod the Great -- 1 BC

> "The view that Herod died in 4 B.C. has been popular for some time, but recent studies indicate that this view is probably wrong and that the traditional date of Herod's death, 1 B.C., is correct."

Akin rejects the widely-held 4 BC date for Herod's death, citing Jack Finegan's *Handbook of Biblical Chronology* (2nd ed.) and Andrew Steinmann's *From Abraham to Paul*. He argues that the 4 BC date rests on "exceptionally weak" arguments and that 1 BC better fits the patristic consensus and the evidence from Josephus. This is significant because Herod's death date is the anchor for dating Jesus's birth: if Herod died in 4 BC, Jesus must have been born by 6-7 BC; if 1 BC, the birth moves to 3-2 BC.

---

## Birth of Jesus -- 3 to 2 BC

> "Consequently, Jesus was not born in 7 or 6 B.C. but in 3 or 2 B.C., as multiple early sources indicate. [...] A large majority of early Christian sources place Jesus' birth at this time."

With Herod's death placed at 1 BC, Akin dates Jesus's birth to late 3 BC or early 2 BC. He notes that Matthew's account of the slaughter of infants "two years old and under" (Matt. 2:16) need not push the birth back more than one or two years before Herod's death. He also connects Luke's reference to a census/enrollment to an empire-wide loyalty oath in 3/2 BC, rather than the later Quirinius census of AD 6-7. A "startling consensus" among early Church Fathers (Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Eusebius, and others) independently places the birth in this same 3-2 BC window.

---

## Ministry of John the Baptist Begins -- AD 29

> "In Luke's gospel, Luke tells us that the ministry of John the Baptist began in the 15th year of Tiberius Caesar. [...] Tiberius' first year, you can treat it for practical purposes as being AD 15. So if you then fast-forward 14 more years from his first year to get to his 15th year, that tells you that the 15th year of Tiberius Caesar is basically AD 29."

Akin treats Luke 3:1 as a precise chronological anchor. Augustus died in August AD 14; the Roman Senate appointed Tiberius in September AD 14. Whether counted from accession or the next January 1, the 15th year of Tiberius falls around AD 29 (possibly late AD 28). Since Jesus was baptized by John and began his ministry shortly afterward, AD 29 is the approximate start of Jesus's public ministry.

---

## Ministry of Jesus -- AD 29 to AD 33

> "John's Gospel refers to multiple Passovers, indicating that Jesus' ministry spanned more than two years -- a better fit for A.D. 33."

The Gospel of John mentions at least three Passovers during Jesus's ministry (John 2:13, 6:4, 11:55), requiring a ministry of at least two-plus years. If the ministry began around AD 29, a span of roughly three to four years places the crucifixion in AD 33 rather than AD 30. An AD 30 crucifixion would compress the ministry to approximately one year, which is difficult to reconcile with John's multiple-Passover framework.

---

## Crucifixion of Jesus -- April 3, AD 33

> "Only two years in that window align a Friday with Passover: April 7, A.D. 30, and April 3, A.D. 33. [...] Taken together, Akin argued, these seven clues point to 3 p.m. on Friday, April 3, A.D. 33."

Akin's seven clues narrow the date as follows: (1) High priesthood of Caiaphas: AD 18-36. (2) Governorship of Pontius Pilate: AD 26-36. (3) 15th year of Tiberius / start of ministry: AD 29. (4) Crucifixion on a Friday ("day of preparation"). (5) Crucifixion at Passover (Nisan 14/15). (6) Multi-year ministry (from John's multiple Passovers). (7) Death at "the ninth hour" (3 p.m.). Only April 7, AD 30 and April 3, AD 33 satisfy clues 4-5 within the AD 29-36 window. Clue 6 favors AD 33. Astronomer Humphreys and Waddington's 1983 *Nature* article confirmed that Nisan 14 fell on Friday, April 3, AD 33.

**Note on the Sabbath question:** Akin holds that the "day of preparation" refers to a regular Friday before the weekly Saturday sabbath, not a preparation day before a high holy day that could fall on any day of the week. This is the traditional Catholic position. Some scholars argue the "high day" sabbath of John 19:31 may indicate a special Passover sabbath distinct from the weekly sabbath, which would permit a Wednesday or Thursday crucifixion. Akin dismisses this interpretation.

## Summary

| Date | Event | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 3-2 BC | Birth of Jesus | Based on Herod's death in 1 BC; supported by patristic consensus |
| 1 BC | Death of Herod the Great | Rejects popular 4 BC date; follows Finegan and Steinmann |
| AD 29 | Start of John the Baptist's ministry | 15th year of Tiberius (Luke 3:1) |
| AD 29 | Start of Jesus's ministry | Shortly after John's; baptism in the Jordan |
| April 3, AD 33 | Crucifixion of Jesus | Friday, Nisan 14, 3 p.m.; 7 clues converge |

Akin's chronology places the crucifixion within the broader New Testament timeline as follows: a birth in 3-2 BC aligns with the census/oath under Augustus, the flight to Egypt, and return after Herod's death in 1 BC. A ministry beginning in AD 29 allows roughly 3.5 years of public teaching, consistent with John's three-plus Passovers. The April 3, AD 33 crucifixion date is also significant for Daniel's 70 weeks prophecy: depending on which decree is used as the starting point (particularly Artaxerxes to Nehemiah in 445-444 BC or Artaxerxes to Ezra in 458-457 BC), a span of 483 years (69 weeks of years) can be calculated to arrive in the early AD 30s, potentially intersecting with this crucifixion date. This makes the dating relevant not only to the Passion narrative but to the broader prophetic timeline examined elsewhere in this repository.
