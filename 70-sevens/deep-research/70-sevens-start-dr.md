# Start Dates for Daniel’s 70 Weeks of Years Prophecy (Daniel 9:24–27): Historical, Textual, and Scholarly Analysis

---

## Summary

The prophecy of Daniel’s “seventy weeks” (Daniel 9:24–27) outlines a 490-year period culminating in the arrival and death of an “anointed one” and the restoration of Jerusalem. Central to interpreting this prophecy is identifying the starting point of the timeline—“the issuing of the word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem.” Scholars have proposed five main candidates:

- **Jeremiah’s Prophecy (609–605 BCE):** Viewed by some Jewish and critical scholars as the prophetic “word” initiating the seventy weeks. However, it lacks an explicit decree to rebuild Jerusalem, and the chronology does not align well with key historical events, making it a minority view.
- **Cyrus’s Decree (539–538 BCE):** Authorized the rebuilding of the temple, not the city. While supported by biblical texts (Ezra, Isaiah) and extra-biblical sources (Cyrus Cylinder, Josephus), the timeline does not align precisely with messianic events, and the city-focused requirement of Daniel 9:25 is not clearly met.
- **Darius I’s Decree (520–519 BCE):** Reaffirms Cyrus’s temple-focused decree. It lacks new authorization for city rebuilding and does not align chronologically with messianic expectations, making it a marginal view.
- **Artaxerxes’ Decree to Ezra (458–457 BCE):** Grants religious and civil authority but lacks explicit mention of rebuilding Jerusalem’s walls. Counting 483 years from this date leads to ~27 CE, aligning with Jesus’s ministry. Favored by many 19th-century Protestant scholars.
- **Artaxerxes’ Decree to Nehemiah (445–444 BCE):** Explicitly authorizes the rebuilding of Jerusalem’s walls and infrastructure. Using a 360-day “prophetic year,” 483 years from 444 BCE leads to 33 CE, aligning with Jesus’s crucifixion. This is the dominant view among modern evangelical scholars due to its textual clarity and chronological fit.

Overall, the Nehemiah decree is most widely accepted for its explicit city-focused mandate and alignment with New Testament events, though alternative views persist based on theological, symbolic, or historical considerations.

---

## Introduction

The prophecy of the “seventy weeks” in Daniel 9:24–27 stands as one of the most debated chronological texts in biblical literature. Its interpretation has profound implications for Jewish and Christian eschatology, messianic expectation, and the historical reconstruction of the Second Temple period. Central to the debate is the identification of the prophecy’s starting point—the “issuing of the word [davar] to restore and rebuild Jerusalem.” Over the centuries, interpreters have proposed several possible start dates, each grounded in different historical decrees, biblical passages, and linguistic nuances. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the major proposed starting points: Jeremiah’s prophecy (609–605 BCE), Cyrus’s decree (539–538 BCE), Darius’s decree (520–519 BCE), Artaxerxes’ decree to Ezra (458–457 BCE), and Artaxerxes’ decree to Nehemiah (445–444 BCE). For each, we examine the biblical context, linguistic and philological considerations, extra-biblical historical sources, archaeological evidence, and the spectrum of scholarly opinion. Special attention is given to the distinction between temple-focused and city-focused decrees, the meaning of key Hebrew terms, and the methodological challenges of prophetic chronology.

## 1. The Prophecy of Daniel 9:24–27: Textual and Historical Context

### 1.1 The Structure and Content of the Prophecy

Daniel 9:24–27 presents a prophecy delivered to Daniel by the angel Gabriel in response to Daniel’s prayer concerning the end of Jerusalem’s desolations. The prophecy divides seventy “weeks” (shabu‘im)—understood as seventy periods of seven years, or 490 years—into three segments: seven weeks (49 years), sixty-two weeks (434 years), and a final week (7 years). The prophecy outlines a sequence of events: the restoration and rebuilding of Jerusalem, the coming and “cutting off” of an “anointed one” (mashiach), the destruction of the city and sanctuary, and a final period of covenant and desolation.

### 1.2 The Immediate Literary Context: Daniel’s Prayer and Jeremiah’s Seventy Years

Daniel’s prayer (Daniel 9:1–19) is explicitly prompted by his reading of Jeremiah’s prophecy of seventy years of Babylonian domination and Jerusalem’s desolation (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). Daniel recognizes that the seventy years are nearing completion and pleads for God’s mercy and restoration. The angelic response reframes the seventy years as “seventy weeks” of years, extending the horizon from the immediate post-exilic restoration to a much longer period of redemptive history.

### 1.3 The Key Terminology: *Davar*, *Shabu‘a*, *Mashiach*, and Related Terms

- **Davar (דָּבָר):** In Daniel 9:25, the prophecy begins “from the issuing of the word [davar] to restore and rebuild Jerusalem.” The term *davar* is semantically broad, encompassing “word,” “matter,” “thing,” “event,” or “decree.” In biblical usage, *davar* can refer to divine speech, prophetic utterance, or a royal edict, but is not the standard technical term for a Persian decree (which would be *ta‘am* or *kol*).
- **Shabu‘a (שָׁבוּעַ):** The word translated “week” literally means “a period of seven,” and in Daniel 9 is universally understood as a heptad of years, not days.
- **Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ):** The term “anointed one” is used without the definite article, allowing for a range of referents: a king, priest, or other divinely appointed figure. Its application in Daniel 9 is debated—whether it refers to Cyrus, a high priest, or the expected Messiah.

## 2. Proposed Starting Points: Historical and Textual Analysis

### 2.1 Jeremiah’s Prophecy as Starting Point (609–605 BCE)

#### 2.1.1 Biblical and Linguistic Basis

Some interpreters, especially within Jewish and critical traditions, propose that the “word” (davar) refers not to a Persian royal decree but to the prophetic word given through Jeremiah concerning the seventy years of Babylonian domination (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). Daniel 9:2 itself references Daniel’s study of “the word [davar] of the LORD given to Jeremiah the prophet”.

#### 2.1.2 Chronological Calculations

- **First Deportation (605 BCE):** The seventy years are sometimes reckoned from the first Babylonian deportation (605 BCE) to the initial return under Cyrus (537/536 BCE), yielding a literal seventy-year span.
- **Babylonian Hegemony (609 BCE):** Others begin the seventy years with Babylon’s defeat of Assyria and regional ascendancy (609 BCE), ending with Babylon’s fall to Cyrus in 539 BCE.

#### 2.1.3 Extra-Biblical Corroboration

- **Babylonian Chronicle and Nabonidus Chronicle:** These cuneiform records confirm the sequence of Babylonian and Persian conquests, the fall of Jerusalem (586 BCE), and the entry of Cyrus into Babylon (539 BCE).
- **Josephus:** The Jewish historian affirms the seventy-year captivity and links Cyrus’s decree to the fulfillment of Jeremiah’s prophecy.

#### 2.1.4 Scholarly and Interpretive Reception

- **Majority View:** Most modern scholars do not accept Jeremiah’s prophecy as the starting point for Daniel’s seventy weeks, since the text of Daniel 9:25 specifies a “word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem,” which is not explicit in Jeremiah’s oracles.
- **Minority and Jewish View:** Some Jewish interpreters and critical scholars maintain that the seventy weeks are a symbolic extension of Jeremiah’s seventy years, beginning with the destruction of the First Temple (586 BCE) and culminating in the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE).

#### 2.1.5 Temple vs. City Focus

Jeremiah’s prophecy is primarily concerned with the desolation and restoration of the land and city, but does not specify a decree to rebuild Jerusalem’s walls or temple.

#### 2.1.6 Summary

While the linguistic link between *davar* and Jeremiah’s prophecy is strong, the lack of an explicit “decree” to rebuild Jerusalem in Jeremiah, and the chronological challenges of fitting 490 years between 605/586 BCE and the first century CE, make this a minority position in both Jewish and Christian scholarship.

---

### 2.2 Cyrus’s Decree as Starting Point (539–538 BCE): Temple-Focused Interpretation

#### 2.2.1 Biblical and Linguistic Basis

- **Ezra 1:1–4:** Records Cyrus’s proclamation (kol) allowing the Jewish exiles to return and rebuild the “house of the LORD” in Jerusalem. The decree is confirmed in Ezra 6:3–5, where a scroll is found in Ecbatana detailing Cyrus’s authorization and financial support for the temple’s reconstruction.
- **Isaiah 44:28; 45:13:** Isaiah prophesies that Cyrus will “say of Jerusalem, ‘Let it be rebuilt,’ and of the temple, ‘Let its foundations be laid.’” This is cited as evidence that Cyrus’s decree encompassed both temple and city restoration.
- **Josephus:** Preserves a version of Cyrus’s letter that includes permission to “rebuild their city, and to build the temple of God at Jerusalem on the same place where it was before”.

#### 2.2.2 Extra-Biblical Historical Sources

- **Cyrus Cylinder:** This Akkadian inscription describes Cyrus’s policy of repatriating displaced peoples and restoring sanctuaries, though it does not mention Jerusalem or the Jews by name. It corroborates the general Persian policy of religious restoration.
- **Babylonian Chronicle and Nabonidus Chronicle:** Confirm the fall of Babylon to Cyrus in 539 BCE and the subsequent administrative changes.

#### 2.2.3 Archaeological and Administrative Evidence

- **Ecbatana Archives:** The discovery of a scroll in Ecbatana (Ezra 6) aligns with Persian archival practices, as confirmed by archaeological finds of administrative texts stored in clay jars or wooden boxes.
- **Murashu Tablets:** These business records from Nippur document Jewish families active in Babylonian society during the Persian period, illustrating the context of the return and the economic realities of those who remained.

#### 2.2.4 Temple vs. City Focus

- **Temple-Focused:** The biblical text of Cyrus’s decree (Ezra 1, 6) is explicitly focused on the rebuilding of the temple, not the city walls or broader urban infrastructure. This is a key argument against identifying Cyrus’s decree as the starting point for Daniel 9:25, which specifies the restoration and rebuilding of Jerusalem itself.
- **City-Focused (Josephus, Isaiah):** Some argue, based on Josephus and Isaiah, that Cyrus’s decree implicitly or explicitly included the city’s restoration.

#### 2.2.5 Chronological Calculations

- **483 Years from 538 BCE:** Counting 483 years from 538 BCE yields a terminus around 55 BCE, which does not correspond to any major messianic or redemptive event in Jewish or Christian chronology. Some propose approximate fulfillment, aligning the period with the general era of the Second Temple and the rise of messianic expectation.

#### 2.2.6 Scholarly and Interpretive Reception

- **Majority View:** Most evangelical and critical scholars reject Cyrus’s decree as the starting point, citing its temple focus and the lack of precise chronological correspondence with the coming of the Messiah.
- **Minority View:** Some, including John Calvin and recent proponents of “approximate fulfillment,” argue for Cyrus’s decree as the most natural starting point, given the context of Daniel’s prayer and the explicit connection to Jeremiah’s seventy years.

#### 2.2.7 Manuscript and Textual Evidence

- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** The Daniel manuscripts from Qumran (e.g., 4QDanc) attest to the stability of the text and its circulation before the first century BCE, supporting the antiquity of the seventy weeks prophecy.

#### 2.2.8 Summary

Cyrus’s decree is well-attested historically and marks the end of the Babylonian exile, but its temple focus and the lack of precise chronological fulfillment for the 69 weeks make it a minority view among modern scholars. Some, however, defend it on contextual and theological grounds, emphasizing the continuity between Jeremiah’s seventy years and Daniel’s seventy weeks.

---

### 2.3 Darius I’s Decree as Starting Point (520–519 BCE): Confirmation of Cyrus

#### 2.3.1 Biblical and Historical Basis

- **Ezra 6:1–12:** Darius I reaffirms Cyrus’s decree after a search of the archives, authorizing the completion of the temple and providing imperial support and protection for the builders. The temple is completed in the sixth year of Darius (515 BCE).
- **Prophetic Encouragement:** The prophets Haggai and Zechariah urge the people to resume work on the temple, linking the decree to divine providence and the fulfillment of prophecy.

#### 2.3.2 Extra-Biblical Corroboration

- **Behistun Inscription:** Chronicles Darius’s consolidation of power and administrative reforms, but does not mention Jerusalem or the Jewish return directly.
- **Persepolis Fortification Archive:** Documents the administrative and economic practices of the Persian court, illustrating the broader context of imperial support for local religious projects.

#### 2.3.3 Temple vs. City Focus

- **Temple-Focused:** Like Cyrus’s decree, Darius’s edict is explicitly concerned with the temple’s completion, not the rebuilding of Jerusalem’s walls or urban infrastructure.

#### 2.3.4 Chronological Calculations

- **483 Years from 520 BCE:** Counting 483 years from 520 BCE yields a terminus around 37 BCE, which again does not align with major messianic events.

#### 2.3.5 Scholarly and Interpretive Reception

- **Minority View:** Few scholars advocate for Darius’s decree as the starting point, as it is seen as a confirmation of Cyrus’s earlier edict rather than a new, city-focused decree.

#### 2.3.6 Summary

Darius’s decree played a crucial role in the completion of the Second Temple and the reestablishment of Jewish worship, but its temple focus and lack of chronological fit with Daniel’s prophecy render it a minority position.

---

### 2.4 Artaxerxes’ Decree to Ezra as Starting Point (458–457 BCE): Religious and Administrative Restoration

#### 2.4.1 Biblical and Historical Basis

- **Ezra 7:11–26:** In the seventh year of Artaxerxes I (458/457 BCE), the king issues a letter authorizing Ezra to lead a group of exiles to Jerusalem, appoint magistrates and judges, and provide resources for temple worship. The decree grants Ezra significant religious and civil authority, including the enforcement of the Law of Moses.

#### 2.4.2 Extra-Biblical Corroboration

- **Ptolemy’s Canon:** Provides a reliable chronological framework for the reigns of Persian kings, confirming the date of Artaxerxes I’s accession and regnal years.
- **Elephantine Papyri:** Aramaic documents from the Jewish community in Egypt attest to Persian administrative practices and the authority of Jewish leaders under Persian rule.

#### 2.4.3 Temple vs. City Focus

- **Mixed Focus:** The decree is primarily concerned with religious and temple matters, but also grants Ezra authority to appoint civil officials and enforce the law. Ezra’s prayer (Ezra 9:9) thanks God for giving “a wall in Judah and Jerusalem,” which some interpret as a metaphor for divine protection, while others see it as a reference to actual city walls.

#### 2.4.4 Chronological Calculations

- **483 Years from 457 BCE:** Counting 483 solar years from 457 BCE yields a terminus around 27 CE, which aligns closely with the beginning of Jesus’s public ministry, according to some chronologies. Using a 360-day “prophetic year,” the terminus falls slightly later, around 26–33 CE.

#### 2.4.5 Scholarly and Interpretive Reception

- **Majority View (19th–Early 20th Century):** Many Protestant and evangelical scholars, including Pusey, Prideaux, Stackhouse, Kett, and Cresswell, favored the decree to Ezra as the starting point, seeing it as the most comprehensive authorization for the restoration of Jewish religious and civil life.
- **Modern Evangelical and Critical Views:** Some continue to support this date, while others prefer the later decree to Nehemiah for its explicit city-building focus.

#### 2.4.6 Manuscript and Textual Evidence

- **Septuagint and Masoretic Text:** Both preserve the text of Ezra’s decree, with minor variations.

#### 2.4.7 Summary

Artaxerxes’ decree to Ezra marks a significant moment in the restoration of Jewish religious and civil life, with some city-focused elements. Its chronological fit with the beginning of Jesus’s ministry makes it attractive to some interpreters, though its lack of explicit authorization for rebuilding Jerusalem’s walls is a point of contention.

---

### 2.5 Artaxerxes’ Decree to Nehemiah as Starting Point (445–444 BCE): City-Focused Interpretation

#### 2.5.1 Biblical and Historical Basis

- **Nehemiah 2:1–8:** In the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I (445/444 BCE), Nehemiah, the king’s cupbearer, receives permission to return to Jerusalem and rebuild its walls and gates. The king provides letters for safe passage and timber for the gates, walls, and Nehemiah’s residence. Nehemiah is appointed governor and oversees the rapid reconstruction of the city’s fortifications, completing the work in 52 days despite opposition.

#### 2.5.2 Extra-Biblical Corroboration

- **Ptolemy’s Canon and Babylonian Business Tablets:** Confirm the regnal years of Artaxerxes I and synchronize with the biblical chronology.
- **Elephantine Papyri:** Reference appeals to “King Artaxerxes” for religious and administrative permissions, illustrating the Persian policy of authorizing local restoration projects.
- **Persepolis Fortification Archive:** Documents the administrative style and resource distribution of Artaxerxes’ reign, supporting the plausibility of royal timber grants and bureaucratic procedures described in Nehemiah.

#### 2.5.3 Archaeological Evidence

- **Jerusalem Excavations:** Archaeological work in the City of David and Ophel has uncovered fortifications and wall repairs consistent with the biblical account of Nehemiah’s rebuilding efforts in the mid-fifth century BCE.

#### 2.5.4 Temple vs. City Focus

- **City-Focused:** Nehemiah’s commission is unique among the Persian decrees in its explicit authorization to rebuild Jerusalem’s walls and urban infrastructure. The text repeatedly emphasizes the restoration of the city’s defenses and gates, fulfilling the criteria of Daniel 9:25 for a “word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem”.

#### 2.5.5 Chronological Calculations

- **483 Years from 444 BCE:** Using a 360-day “prophetic year” (173,880 days), 483 years from 444 BCE brings the terminus to 33 CE, aligning with the traditional date of Jesus’s crucifixion. This calculation, popularized by Sir Robert Anderson and refined by Harold Hoehner, is widely cited in evangelical literature.
- **Solar Year Calculation:** Using 365.24-day years, the terminus falls slightly later, but still within the range of Jesus’s public ministry and death.

#### 2.5.6 Regnal Year Reckoning and Calendar Systems

- **Persian Accession/Non-Accession Systems:** The calculation of Artaxerxes’ regnal years depends on whether the accession year is counted (accession-year system) or not (non-accession-year system). Most modern chronologists use the Persian spring-to-spring (Nisan) calendar, placing Nehemiah’s commission in Nisan 444 BCE.

#### 2.5.7 Scholarly and Interpretive Reception

- **Majority Modern Evangelical View:** The decree to Nehemiah is the most widely accepted starting point among conservative Protestant and evangelical scholars, including Sir Robert Anderson, Harold Hoehner, Edwin Thiele, and many others.
- **Critical and Jewish Views:** Some critical scholars and Jewish interpreters reject the messianic application of the prophecy, or prefer earlier decrees for theological or chronological reasons.

#### 2.5.8 Manuscript and Textual Evidence

- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** The Daniel manuscripts from Qumran preserve Daniel 9 with remarkable fidelity, supporting the textual stability of the prophecy.

#### 2.5.9 Summary

Artaxerxes’ decree to Nehemiah is the only Persian edict that explicitly authorizes the rebuilding of Jerusalem’s walls and urban infrastructure. Its chronological fit with the life and death of Jesus, when calculated using “prophetic years,” has made it the dominant view among modern evangelical interpreters.

## 3. Linguistic and Philological Considerations

### 3.1 The Meaning and Range of *Davar* (דָּבָר)

- **Semantic Range:** *Davar* encompasses “word,” “matter,” “thing,” “event,” or “decree.” In Daniel 9:25, it is used for the “issuing of the word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem.” While *davar* can refer to a royal decree (cf. Esther 1:19), it is not the standard technical term for a Persian edict, which would be *ta‘am* or *kol*.
- **Interpretive Implications:** The use of *davar* allows for both a divine or prophetic word (as in Jeremiah) and a royal decree (as in the Persian edicts). The context of Daniel 9:25, however, suggests a public, authoritative command that initiates the restoration of Jerusalem.

### 3.2 The Philology of *Shabu‘a* (שָׁבוּעַ)

- **Heptad of Years:** In Daniel 9, *shabu‘a* is universally understood as a period of seven years, based on parallels in Leviticus 25:8 and the structure of sabbatical and jubilee cycles.

### 3.3 The Syntax of *Mashiach* (מָשִׁיחַ) and *Nagid* (נָגִיד)

- **Anointed One, Prince:** The phrase “an anointed one, a prince” lacks the definite article, allowing for multiple referents (Cyrus, a high priest, or the Messiah). The identification depends on the interpreter’s broader theological and historical framework.

## 4. Calendar Systems, Regnal Year Reckoning, and Chronological Calculations

### 4.1 Calendar Systems

- **Solar vs. Lunar Years:** The ancient Near East used both lunar-solar calendars (Babylonian, Persian) and schematic “prophetic years” of 360 days (twelve 30-day months). The latter is favored by some interpreters for prophetic chronology, based on parallels in Genesis and Revelation.

### 4.2 Regnal Year Reckoning

- **Accession vs. Non-Accession Year:** Persian kings sometimes counted their regnal years from the first Nisan after accession (accession-year system), while other systems counted the accession year as year one (non-accession-year system). The choice affects the dating of Artaxerxes’ twentieth year and the calculation of Nehemiah’s commission.

### 4.3 Counting Methods for the 69/70 Weeks

- **Inclusive Reckoning:** Some calculations count both the starting and ending years (inclusive reckoning), while others use exclusive reckoning.
- **Sabbatical Cycles and Jubilee Theory:** Some propose that the seventy weeks correspond to ten jubilee cycles (49 years each), aligning the prophecy with Israel’s sabbatical rhythms.

### 4.4 Chronological Calculations: Prophetic vs. Solar Years

- **Prophetic Year (360 Days):** 483 prophetic years = 173,880 days. Counting from Nisan 444 BCE (Artaxerxes’ decree to Nehemiah), this brings the terminus to Nisan 33 CE, aligning with Jesus’s crucifixion.
- **Solar Year (365.24 Days):** 483 solar years from 444 BCE yields a terminus around 40 CE, slightly later than the traditional date of Jesus’s death.

## 5. Manuscript Evidence and Textual Transmission

### 5.1 Dead Sea Scrolls

- **Daniel Manuscripts:** The Qumran Daniel scrolls (e.g., 4QDanc) date to the second century BCE and preserve Daniel 9 with remarkable fidelity, demonstrating the antiquity and stability of the seventy weeks prophecy.

### 5.2 Septuagint and Masoretic Text

- **Textual Variants:** The Septuagint and Masoretic Text differ slightly in the division of the weeks and the punctuation of Daniel 9:25–26, affecting the interpretation of the “anointed one” and the sequence of events.

---

## 6. Archaeological and Extra-Biblical Historical Evidence

### 6.1 Babylonian and Persian Chronicles

- **Babylonian Chronicle and Nabonidus Chronicle:** Provide independent confirmation of the fall of Babylon, the reigns of Persian kings, and the general historical framework of the exile and return.
- **Cyrus Cylinder:** Corroborates Cyrus’s policy of repatriation and temple restoration, though it does not mention Jerusalem specifically.

### 6.2 Josephus and Classical Historiography

- **Antiquities of the Jews:** Josephus preserves versions of Persian decrees, including Cyrus’s letter authorizing the rebuilding of the city and temple, and provides a first-century Jewish perspective on the fulfillment of Jeremiah’s prophecy.

### 6.3 Elephantine Papyri

- **Jewish Community in Egypt:** The Elephantine documents attest to the administrative practices of the Persian empire and the autonomy granted to Jewish communities, supporting the plausibility of the biblical accounts of Ezra and Nehemiah.

### 6.4 Murashu Tablets and Persepolis Fortification Archive

- **Economic and Administrative Records:** These archives illustrate the integration of Jewish families into Persian society and the administrative mechanisms of the empire, providing context for the biblical narratives.

### 6.5 Archaeological Evidence in Jerusalem

- **City Walls and Fortifications:** Excavations in the City of David and Ophel have uncovered remains of walls and gates consistent with Nehemiah’s rebuilding efforts in the fifth century BCE.

## 7. Interpretive Traditions and Scholarly Reception

### 7.1 Jewish, Patristic, Medieval, and Reformation Views

- **Jewish Tradition:** The Seder Olam Rabbah dates the seventy weeks from the destruction of the First Temple (421 BCE) to the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE), interpreting the prophecy as fulfilled in the events of the Second Temple period.
- **Patristic and Medieval Christian Tradition:** Early church fathers and medieval commentators offered a range of interpretations, often linking the prophecy to the coming of Christ but differing on the starting point and the identity of the “anointed one”.
- **Reformation and Modern Evangelical Tradition:** The dominant view since the nineteenth century has been to identify the decree to Nehemiah (444 BCE) as the starting point, aligning the 69 weeks with the life and death of Jesus.

### 7.2 Critical and Historicist Approaches

- **Critical Scholarship:** Many critical scholars view the seventy weeks as a symbolic period, constructed retrospectively to fit the events of the Maccabean crisis or the Second Temple period, rather than as a predictive prophecy.
- **Historicist and Preterist Approaches:** Some interpreters see the seventy weeks as a continuous period fulfilled in the past, while futurists posit a gap between the 69th and 70th weeks, projecting the final week into the eschatological future.

### 7.3 Methodological Issues in Prophetic Chronology

- **Literal vs. Approximate Fulfillment:** Some scholars argue for literal, mathematically precise fulfillment of the 483 years, while others advocate for approximate fulfillment, emphasizing the symbolic and theological dimensions of the prophecy.
- **Use of Prophetic Years:** The adoption of a 360-day “prophetic year” is debated, with some arguing for its scriptural basis and others rejecting it as an artificial construct.

## 9. Detailed Comparative Analysis

### 9.1 Temple-Focused vs. City-Focused Decrees

- **Temple-Focused:** The decrees of Cyrus and Darius are explicitly concerned with the rebuilding and support of the temple. While the restoration of worship and the return of exiles are central, there is no explicit authorization for rebuilding Jerusalem’s walls or urban infrastructure.
- **City-Focused:** The decree to Nehemiah is unique in its explicit focus on the city’s walls and gates, fulfilling the criteria of Daniel 9:25 for a “word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem.” The decree to Ezra, while granting significant civil authority, is less explicit regarding the city’s physical restoration.

### 9.2 Chronological Alignment with Messianic Events

- **Cyrus’s Decree (538 BCE):** 483 years later = ~55 BCE (no major messianic event).
- **Artaxerxes’ Decree to Ezra (457 BCE):** 483 years later = ~27 CE (beginning of Jesus’s ministry).
- **Artaxerxes’ Decree to Nehemiah (444 BCE):** 483 prophetic years (360 days) later = 33 CE (traditional date of Jesus’s crucifixion).

### 9.3 Majority vs. Minority Scholarly Positions

- **Majority View:** Artaxerxes’ decree to Nehemiah (444 BCE) is the dominant position among modern evangelical and conservative scholars, due to its explicit city focus and chronological fit with the New Testament narrative.
- **Significant Minority:** The decree to Ezra (457 BCE) is supported by many nineteenth-century and some modern scholars, especially those emphasizing religious and civil restoration.
- **Minority Views:** The decrees of Cyrus and Darius, and the prophetic word of Jeremiah, are minority positions, often advocated for theological or contextual reasons rather than strict chronological alignment.

## 10. Conclusion: Interpretive Trends and the State of the Debate

The question of the starting point for Daniel’s seventy weeks prophecy remains one of the most intricate and contested issues in biblical chronology. The evidence marshaled from biblical texts, linguistic analysis, extra-biblical sources, and archaeological discoveries reveals a complex interplay of historical events, theological expectations, and interpretive traditions.

**Key trends and conclusions:**

- **Textual and Contextual Fit:** The majority of modern scholarship favors the decree to Nehemiah (444 BCE) as the starting point, due to its explicit authorization to rebuild Jerusalem’s walls and its alignment with the requirements of Daniel 9:25. This view is especially prominent in evangelical and conservative circles, where the chronological fit with the life and death of Jesus is seen as a powerful apologetic for the reliability of prophecy.
- **Temple vs. City Distinction:** The distinction between temple-focused and city-focused decrees is crucial. While the decrees of Cyrus and Darius were foundational for the restoration of Jewish worship, only Nehemiah’s commission directly addresses the rebuilding of Jerusalem as a city with defenses and infrastructure.
- **Linguistic Nuances:** The use of *davar* in Daniel 9:25 allows for both divine and royal decrees, but the context favors a public, authoritative command that initiates the city’s restoration.
- **Chronological Calculations:** The adoption of a 360-day “prophetic year” is a methodological choice that enables the alignment of the 69 weeks with the New Testament chronology. This approach is debated, with some scholars preferring solar years or approximate fulfillment.
- **Minority and Alternative Views:** Some Jewish and critical scholars maintain that the seventy weeks are a symbolic extension of Jeremiah’s seventy years, beginning with the destruction of the First Temple and culminating in the destruction of the Second Temple. Others advocate for Cyrus’s decree as the most natural contextual starting point, emphasizing theological continuity over chronological precision.
- **Archaeological and Historical Corroboration:** The convergence of biblical, archaeological, and extra-biblical evidence supports the general historical framework of the exile, return, and Persian-era restoration, though precise synchronisms remain debated.

**In summary:** While the decree to Nehemiah in 444 BCE is the majority scholarly position for the starting point of Daniel’s seventy weeks, each proposed date reflects different emphases—textual, theological, chronological, or historical. The debate continues to be shaped by new discoveries, methodological innovations, and the enduring significance of Daniel’s prophecy for Jewish and Christian thought.
